1. A balanced approach

Balance is flexible, not perfect.

Life can feel busy. A balanced approach means making space for the things that give you energy, as well as the things you need to get done.

More energy

Rest, food, movement and enjoyable activities can help your body and brain recharge.

More resilience

When one part of life gets tough, supportive routines and people can help you cope.

More enjoyment

Balance leaves room for the things that matter to you, not only the things on your to-do list.

2. Different elements of wellbeing

Your wellbeing has more than one side.

These areas overlap. Looking after one may support another — for example, good sleep can make it easier to manage emotions and concentrate in class.

Physical

Sleep, food, movement, health and looking after your body.

Emotional

Naming feelings, managing big emotions and being kind to yourself.

Social

Friendships, family, belonging and asking for support when needed.

Learning

Curiosity, skills, achievement and strategies when work is challenging.

Rest & play

Relaxing, hobbies, creativity and time away from pressure.

Environment

Feeling safe, having spaces that work for you and spending time outdoors where possible.

3. Learning supports wellbeing

Learning can build confidence — not just grades.

Learning something new can give you a sense of progress and purpose. It can also help you understand yourself and the world around you.

Practising a skill reminds you that improvement is possible, even when something feels difficult at first.

4. Stress and anxiety

Similar feelings. Different experiences.

Stress and anxiety are common human responses. Understanding them can help you choose a next step and know when to ask for support.

Stress

Stress is often your response to a pressure happening now, such as deadlines, an argument or a busy week.

Try: pause, breathe slowly, break the task into smaller steps and talk through the pressure with someone you trust.

Anxiety

Anxiety is a feeling of worry, fear or unease that can carry on even when there is no clear immediate threat.

Try: name five things you can see, breathe slowly and tell a trusted adult if worries are lasting or getting in the way.
